Explainable AI for Spectroscopic Data

From Black Boxes to Transparent Models in OPTIR and FTIR Spectroscopy

Krzysztof Banas

2026-07-21

Introduction

Why This Talk Matters

  • Spectroscopy generates high-dimensional, information-rich data

  • AI/ML models are increasingly used for classification, quantification, and identification

  • Regulatory and scientific communities demand transparency and reproducibility

  • The gap between model performance and model understanding is widening

FTIR Spectroscopy — A Brief Primer

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

  • Measures absorption of infrared light by molecular bonds

  • Produces a spectrum: absorbance vs. wavenumber (typically 4000–400 cm⁻¹)

  • Functional group identification via characteristic bands (C=O, O–H, N–H, C–H)

  • Widely used in pharmaceuticals, polymers, food science, forensics

OPTIR Spectroscopy — The New Frontier

Optical Photothermal Infrared Spectroscopy

  • Overcomes the diffraction limit of conventional IR microscopy

  • Uses a pulsed, tunable IR laser to heat the sample locally

  • A visible probe beam detects the photothermal response

  • Achieves spatial resolution of ~500 nm (vs. ~10 µm for FTIR microscopy)

  • Ideal for biological tissues, single cells, microplastics, thin films

History of AI in Data Evaluation

The Timeline of AI in Analytical Science

Classical Chemometrics Era

Transparent by Design

  • PCA: Loadings directly map to spectral features

  • PLS/PCR: Regression coefficients interpretable as spectral weights

  • HCA: Dendrograms show grouping logic

  • MCR-ALS: Recovers pure component spectra

Key advantage: Every step is chemically traceable

The Deep Learning Shift

Power at a Cost

  • 1D-CNNs learn hierarchical spectral features automatically

  • Autoencoders perform nonlinear dimensionality reduction

  • Transfer learning enables small-dataset performance

  • Transformers capture long-range spectral dependencies

The cost: Internal representations are not human-readable

Why Black Boxes Are Dangerous

The Black Box Problem Defined

A black box model is one where:

  • Inputs and outputs are known

  • The internal decision-making process is opaque

  • Users cannot determine why a specific prediction was made

  • There is no way to verify if the model uses chemically valid reasoning

In Spectroscopy This Means:

“The model says this is Protein A, but we cannot tell if it used the Amide I band or a baseline artefact to decide.”

Real-World Risks of Opaque Models

Documented Failures

  1. Spurious correlations: Models classifying tissue types based on sample preparation artefacts rather than biochemistry

  2. Confounding variables: Instrument-specific features mistaken for sample differences

  3. Shortcut learning: Models exploiting label leakage in spectral datasets

  4. Distribution shift: Models failing silently on data from new instruments or protocols

The Regulatory Imperative

Compliance Demands Transparency

  • EU AI Act (2024): High-risk AI systems require explainability
  • FDA 21 CFR Part 11: Analytical methods must be validated and understood
  • ISO/IEC 42001: AI management systems demand risk assessment

The Scientific Method Requires It Too

  • Reproducibility requires understanding what a model does
  • Peer review cannot evaluate what it cannot see
  • Knowledge generation demands mechanistic insight

Explainable AI: Methods & Tools

XAI Taxonomy for Spectroscopy

SHAP for Spectral Interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations

  • Assigns each wavenumber a contribution to the prediction

  • Based on coalitional game theory — fair attribution

  • Produces spectral importance maps that align with chemical knowledge

  • Can reveal if a model focuses on:

    • ✅ Amide I/II bands (meaningful)
    • ❌ CO₂ region at 2349 cm⁻¹ (artefact)
    • ❌ Baseline slope (instrument noise)

Threats & Ethical Considerations

Now let us turn to the broader ethical landscape. Even when we use explainable models, there are significant threats and ethical considerations that the spectroscopy community must confront.

Bias in Spectroscopic AI

Sources of Bias

  • Dataset bias: Training sets dominated by specific populations, instruments, or sample preparations
  • Label bias: Expert annotations reflecting individual diagnostic tendencies
  • Measurement bias: Systematic differences between instruments, labs, or protocols
  • Selection bias: Published models trained on curated “clean” datasets that do not represent real-world variability

Bias in Spectroscopic AI

Consequences

  • Models that perform well in one lab but fail in another
  • Diagnostic models biased toward specific demographics
  • Overconfident predictions on out-of-distribution samples

Environmental & Societal Costs

Carbon Footprint of AI

  • Training a large transformer model can emit >300 tonnes of CO₂

  • Hyperparameter searches multiply this by 10–100×

  • Spectroscopic foundation models require massive computational resources

The Proportionality Question

  • Is a 2% accuracy improvement worth weeks of GPU time?
  • Should we prefer efficient, interpretable models when they perform comparably?

Environmental & Societal Costs

Data Sovereignty & Privacy

  • Patient spectral data as personal health information
  • Cross-border data flows for cloud-based AI processing
  • Proprietary spectral libraries and intellectual property concerns

Reproducibility & Trust Erosion

The Reproducibility Crisis Meets AI

  • Many published spectroscopic AI models cannot be reproduced

  • Common issues:

    • Missing preprocessing details
    • Unreported data splits
    • No code or model availability
    • Hardware-dependent results

Reproducibility & Trust Erosion

Trust Erosion

  • Clinicians will not adopt tools they do not understand
  • Regulators will not approve methods they cannot audit
  • Scientists should not publish results they cannot explain

The Path Forward

  • Open models, open data, open code
  • Standardised reporting (model cards, datasheets)
  • Mandatory XAI for high-stakes applications

Conclusions & Recommendations

Key Takeaways

Principles

  1. Accuracy without understanding is insufficient for scientific applications

  2. Explainability is not optional — it is a scientific and regulatory requirement

  3. OPTIR and FTIR data are ideal testbeds for XAI because chemical ground truth exists

  4. Bias, carbon cost, and reproducibility are ethical imperatives, not afterthoughts

Recommendations

  1. Always apply at least one XAI method (SHAP, LIME, or gradient-based)

  2. Validate explanations against known spectral assignments

  3. Report model cards with every publication

  4. Prefer interpretable models when performance is comparable

  5. Consider environmental cost in model selection

  6. Demand open data and open code
